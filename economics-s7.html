    GDP and Economic Growth

    How rich is a country, and why? First we learn to measure it properly, then we see how small differences in growth turn into enormous differences in how people live, and finally we dig for the causes.

    1. What goes into GDP

    Picture the whole economy as one giant supermarket. GDP is what the register rings up in a year.

    Gross Domestic Product國內生產毛額 is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. The register follows four rules, and almost every GDP question tests one of them.

    Final only

    Flour a bakery uses is already inside the bread's price. Count the bread, not the flour.

    Capital goods count

    A tractor helps make other goods, but it is not sold again as part of them, so it counts.

    New production only

    A used car or an old house was counted when it was made. Reselling it adds nothing.

    Inside the borders

    A Korean-owned factory in Chonburi adds to Thai GDP. Income Thais earn abroad belongs in GNI instead.

    Spending is split into four buckets: Y = C + I + G + NX, where net exports are exports minus imports. Imports come off because they were made abroad, not because imports are bad. Pension checks and other transfers移轉支付 buy nothing, so they stay off the register until someone spends them.

    PartDefinitionExampleDo not confuse with
    C ConsumptionHousehold spending on final goods and services; the largest part, about 63% of US GDP.Groceries, phone plans, a massage.Buying used goods.
    I InvestmentNew capital goods, usually bought by firms.Machines, tractors, a new warehouse.Buying shares or bonds.
    G Government purchasesGoods and services the government buys directly.A rail line, teachers' salaries, office supplies.Transfers such as pensions, which buy nothing.
    NX Net exportsExports minus imports.Tourists paying Phuket hotels is an export; imported fuel is an import.A sign that imports are bad.

    Why split GDP at all? Households, firms and governments respond to different forces. When GDP falls, it matters whether consumption, investment or government purchases fell, because the policy response is different.

    Amounts are a made-up economy in million baht. The income-side split is illustrative.

    The second bar is the point of the income side. Every baht spent on final output ends up as someone's wages, rent, interest or profit, so adding up incomes (gross domestic income, GDI) should give the same total. In practice the two are measured separately and differ slightly, and in a recession economists watch both.

    A third cut is by sector: who produces the output. Thailand's economy has been shifting toward services.

    2. Three adjustments that make GDP comparable

    Raw GDP answers "how big is this economy?" It does not answer "how well do people live?" Three adjustments close the gap.

    A chart of the ASEAN-6 shows why. Indonesia's economy, at about $1.44 trillion, is more than twice the size of Singapore's $565 billion, Thailand's $538 billion or Vietnam's $514 billion. Yet one reader asked the obvious question underneath: then why are Indonesians still poor compared with Singaporeans? A big total spread across a very large population is not the same as a high income per person.

    The chart shown in class labels Indonesia "$1.443B"; the comments and the bar heights show it means trillion.

    AdjustmentWhat it removesUse it to compare
    Real GDPInflation: every year's output is valued at the same base-year prices.One country over time.
    GDP per capitaDifferences in population size: divide by the number of people.Countries of different sizes.
    Purchasing power parity (PPP)Differences in the cost of living: value every country's output at the same prices.Living standards across countries (section 4).

    Start with the first two. A rising GDP number can be a mirage: prices may have gone up while output stayed put.

    MeasureWhat it doesAnswers the question
    Nominal GDPValues output at this year's prices.How much money changed hands?
    Real GDPValues every year's output at the same base-year prices.Did we produce more?
    GDP deflator平減指數Nominal ÷ real × 100.How much did prices rise overall?
    Real GDP per capitaReal GDP ÷ population.Is the average person better off?

    US figures come from FRED, in 2009 dollars. The per-person rise is 3.6 times on these rounded numbers, often quoted as "about four times".

    A fall in real GDP is part of what defines a recession. In 2009 US real GDP shrank 3.6% and unemployment jumped.

    Quick rule for small rates: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation, and growth per person ≈ real growth − population growth. With rates this large the shortcut drifts.

    3. What GDP per person misses

    It is not a measure of happiness, yet it is still the best single summary we have.

    Why it still works

    The good things in life tend to come together. Countries with higher real GDP per person usually have longer lives, happier people on average, better education, and, over time, higher incomes for the poorest fifth.

    What it leaves out

    Who gets the income, how much leisure people have, unpaid work at home, and damage to the environment. A rise in GDP can even come with more pollution.

    CountryReal GDP per personLiving on under $2 a day
    NigeriaSimilar≈ 80%
    PakistanSimilar≈ 60%
    HondurasSimilar≈ 33%

    The same average hides very different lives. Nigeria has many very poor people and some very rich ones, so the rich pull the average up while most people stay poor. GDP per person tells you how much there is, not who gets it.

    Be careful with cause and effect. Richer countries are healthier, but health also raises productivity, and good institutions can lift both at once.

    4. Comparing countries fairly

    In India an iPhone costs about as much as in the United States, roughly $1,000. A haircut with a head massage costs 50 rupees, under a dollar.

    Tradable goods

    If phones were much cheaper in India, people would buy them there and resell them in the US. That arbitrage pulls prices together everywhere.

    Services that cannot move

    You cannot ship a haircut, and the barber cannot easily move to the US, where he might earn $10 an hour instead of $10 a day. Haircuts, cleaning, taxis and massage stay cheap in poor countries.

    This is the Balassa-Samuelson effect: where factories and offices pay high wages, every job has to pay well to keep its workers, including the barber's. Cheap services make the whole price level lower in poor countries, so a dollar goes further there. Comparing incomes at market exchange rates therefore makes poor countries look poorer than they are. Purchasing power parity購買力平價 (PPP) fixes this by valuing every country's output at the same prices.

    The 2026 world economy shows how much this matters. At market exchange rates the world produces about $128 trillion; at PPP the same output is worth about $219 trillion, because so much of it happens in countries where prices are low.

    IMF estimates for 2026, compiled by Visual Capitalist, as shown in class.

    Why do poorer countries gain the most from the adjustment? The haircut shows the mechanism.

    How to build this graph
    1. Haircuts per day on the horizontal axis, baht per haircut on the vertical axis. Draw D sloping down and S1 sloping up; mark E1, P1, Q1.
    2. A better-paid job elsewhere raises the cost of each haircut, so supply shifts left (up) to S2.
    3. Mark E2: P2 is above P1, Q2 below Q1. If local incomes rise too, shift D right and P climbs further.

    中文邏輯理髮便宜不是因為理髮師比較差，而是整個國家的工資低。可貿易品被套利拉平，服務拉不平，所以要用 PPP 才公平。

    5. How big the gaps are

    Measured at common prices, the gaps between countries are not 20% or 50%. They are 10, 20, sometimes nearly 100 times.

    IMF data, PPP-adjusted: CAR about $600 a year, Mexico $17,800. The US is given as 90 times the CAR, which works out to roughly 540 baskets.

    Two lessons. First, "poor countries" are not one group: Mexico is far closer to the United States than to the Central African Republic, a small landlocked country torn by civil war. Second, these gaps are recent. For most of history average income barely moved; sustained growth began with the Industrial Revolution and then shot up like the blade of a hockey stick.

    6. Why growth rates matter so much

    The United States is rich mainly because it kept growing about 2% a year for two centuries.

    IdeaDefinitionExample
    Compound growth複利Each year's growth builds on last year's higher level.3% a year for 35 years gives 2.8 times, not 2.05.
    Rule of 7070 法則Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate in percent.US income went from about $2,000 in 1845 to $4,000 in 1880: doubled in 35 years, so about 2% a year.
    Ratio scaleEach step on the axis is a doubling, so steady growth is a straight line.On an ordinary scale, steady growth looks like it is speeding up.

    Run the numbers on the US and the stakes are clear. Had it grown only 1% a year since 1800, it would be about as rich today as it was in 1940. Had it grown 3%, it would have reached today's living standard back in 1917, and real GDP per person today would be about $893,000. At current growth rates, it will not get there until 2159.

    Another way to feel the gap is to place today's countries in American history:

    1. 1800Bangladesh and Uganda today
    2. 1880India today
    3. 1917When the US would have reached today's living standard, had it grown 3% a year
    4. 1920sChina today
    5. 1940Where the US would be today, had it grown only 1% a year: few cars, no TV, incomes below today's poverty line
    6. 1980Italy today

    India and China grow fast, so their places keep moving. The comparison is also imperfect: every country today lives longer than the richest countries did in 1800, thanks to vaccines, antibiotics and sanitation spreading from rich countries.

    7. Miracles and disasters

    Since the Industrial Revolution, rich and poor regions have mostly pulled apart. Country by country, though, some have raced ahead and others have stalled.

    JapanArgentina
    1950Poor, about the level of Mexico.Rich, like much of Western Europe; about twice Japan's living standard.
    What happenedDoubled living standards about every 8 years; by the rule of 70, about 8.5% a year.Some years of negative growth; doubled only once in 65 years, about 1.1% a year.
    2015More than 10 times its 1950 level; about twice as rich as Argentina.Stagnated.

    The reversal is just the rule of 70 at work. Several Japanese doublings against one Argentine doubling more than wiped out a two-to-one head start.

    Growth miracle

    Decades of very fast growth per person: Japan, South Korea, China, and perhaps India now. What took the US 200 years of steady growth can happen in about 40.

    Growth disaster

    Long stagnation or decline. Argentina grew for a while, then stalled. Niger and Chad never took off at all. Behind the numbers are shorter, harder lives.

    Why can poor countries grow faster than rich ones? They can copy technology that already exists and add machines where machines are scarce, so each investment pays off a lot. A country already at the frontier has to invent new ideas, which is slower. That is catch-up growth追趕型成長, and it is possible, not automatic. It needs the right conditions, which is where the next section begins.

    8. Why some countries grow: keep asking why

    "Rich countries have more capital" is true but too easy. The useful answer comes from asking why, again and again.

    The answer comes in layers. The most immediate one is that workers in rich countries have more physical capital, more human capital and better technology, organized by entrepreneurs. But that only moves the question back: why did those countries build so much capital? Because people had incentives to work, save and invest. And why the incentives? Because institutions protected the rewards. Why the institutions? That is the least settled part, a mix of history, ideas, culture, geography and luck.

    Five key institutionsWhat it protectsWithout it
    Property rights財產權Your investment.Someone could build on your farm.
    Honest governmentPermits without bribes; no seizure.Bribes become a cost of doing business.
    Political stabilityLong-term plans.Civil war can wash plans away.
    Dependable legal systemContracts, debts, borrowing and lending.Hard to collect a debt or prove who owns what.
    Competitive, open marketsPrice signals and room to innovate.Needless regulations protect monopolies.

    9. Institutions in action

    Two cases at very different scales: a divided country and a single coffee shop.

    One peninsula, two outcomes

    Before 1945 the two Koreas had the same people, language, culture and history, and similar economies; the north was, if anything, richer. Then the North got central planning and the South got a market economy. Tyler Cowen calls it an almost perfect natural experiment.

    South KoreaNorth Korea
    IncentiveServe customers and keep the profit; failing firms close, so capital moves to better uses.No right to start a business or keep profits; prices controlled; capital handed out by the party.
    Where effort goesMaking things people want.Winning favor with officials.
    ResultRich-country living standards; Hyundai cars, K-pop, films and cosmetics sold across Asia and beyond.Repeated famines, because farmers had no reason to grow enough food.
    Seen from space at nightBrightDark, apart from Pyongyang

    Not a perfect experiment: the two halves also had different allies, aid and war damage.

    The road to opening a café in Mumbai

    On paper

    The World Bank once ranked India 130th of 190 for ease of doing business and estimated 14 procedures and 26 days for a simple Mumbai license.

    In practice

    The owner of Koinonia Coffee Roasters said 26 days "would be a dream".

    1. Lease a commercial space before the company even exists, just to have an address to register.
    2. Write down in advance everything the business might ever do; adding anything later means amending the papers.
    3. Work through 10 to 12 licenses with three consultants, some of which changed along the way.
    4. Get a pollution permit for a gas-powered roaster: maps, emissions details and 8 to 12 trips to an office an hour away.
    5. Refuse to pay bribes, and lose a licensing firm that would not work without them.
    6. Sit closed for three months, paying staff and rent, ready to open but waiting for paper.
    More licenses and delays→Costlier, slower entry→Fewer cafés open→Supply shifts left→Higher prices, less output, idle capital

    One honest firm shows bribe-free business is possible, not that it is easy or typical. The World Bank has since stopped publishing Doing Business.

    10. Geography: the natural tariff

    Think of the most prosperous cities you know. Most sit on a coast or a major river.

    Cheap shipping by water→Lower trade costs→Bigger markets→More specialization and new ideas→Growth

    That chain goes back to Adam Smith, and a map of output per square kilometer bears it out: GDP piles up along the US coasts and the Great Lakes, in Western Europe, Japan, coastal China and coastal Australia. Landlocked countries are, on average, poorer. Africa is huge, big enough to hold most of the US, China, India and much of Europe, yet Europe has two to three times more coastline. Africa is the most landlocked continent and has the most poor countries.

    The lesson

    Being landlocked works like a natural tariff天然關稅, a tax on every import and export. If a natural tariff slows growth, a tariff chosen by government is unlikely to help it.

    The limit

    Geography is not destiny. Landlocked Switzerland is rich, and railways and roads can cut the natural tariff. Growth depends on natural advantages and on policy.

    11. When the numbers themselves are weak

    Every comparison above assumes we can count output and people. In the poorest countries, often we cannot.

    The Central African Republic has no reliable birth and death records and no recent census. In 2022 researchers went house to house by car, boat, motorbike and on foot and found that nearly 6% of the population had died that year, mostly from malaria, diarrhea and violence. The UN's figure was about four times lower, because it projected older data forward as if conditions were stable.

    EstimateDeaths per 10,000 people a dayAbove the emergency line of 1?
    Household survey≈ 1.6Yes
    UN estimate≈ 0.4No

    The difference is not academic. Aid agencies treat one death per 10,000 people a day as the threshold for an emergency, so which estimate you believe decides whether the CAR counts as a crisis at all.

    Survey rate converted from nearly 6% of the population a year. The CAR government and the UN's chief demographer disputed the survey, so treat it as evidence of large uncertainty rather than a settled figure.

    The lesson for GDP: the countries at the bottom of every ranking are exactly where both the output figures and the population counts are weakest. "About 90 times richer" is an order of magnitude, not a precise fact.

    12. Do not mix these up

    Anything used to make other goods is intermediate.

    Capital goods such as a tractor are final goods and count.

    GDP counts what citizens produce anywhere.

    That is GNI. GDP counts production inside the borders.

    Investment means buying shares.

    In GDP, investment means new capital goods.

    All government spending is G.

    Transfers such as pensions buy nothing and are left out.

    The US economy grew 55 times since 1950.

    That is nominal. Real is about 8 times; per person about 3.6.

    Indonesia has the biggest economy in ASEAN, so Indonesians are the richest.

    Total GDP measures size. Divide by population to compare living standards.

    Same GDP per person means the same poverty.

    Distribution differs, as Nigeria and Honduras show.

    Haircuts are cheap in India because barbers are worse.

    Wages are low across the economy and workers cannot move.

    Compare incomes at market exchange rates.

    Use PPP, or poor countries look poorer than they are.

    A one-point growth gap is small.

    Over 140 years, 3% against 2% is about 3.9 times.

    Poor countries automatically catch up.

    Argentina, Niger and Chad did not.

    More capital explains why rich countries are rich.

    That is the immediate cause. Ask why the capital was built.

    Geography is destiny.

    It matters, but institutions and infrastructure can offset it.

    13. Cold call practice

    What is GDP?

    The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year, for example cars built in Rayong and massages sold in Bangkok this year.

    Why does a tractor count but a bakery's flour does not?

    The flour is used up inside the bread, whose price already includes it. The tractor is a final capital good that is never resold as part of another product.

    Why are pensions not part of G?

    They are transfers that buy no output. When retirees spend the money it shows up as consumption, so counting the check as well would double count.

    What three adjustments make GDP comparable?

    Real GDP removes inflation, so we can compare over time; GDP per capita removes population size, so we can compare countries of different sizes; PPP removes differences in the cost of living.

    What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

    Nominal GDP uses current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP holds prices fixed, so it rises only when output rises.

    Is real GDP per capita a good measure of living standards?

    It is the best single measure because it moves with life expectancy, happiness and education, but it ignores how income is shared, leisure and the environment.

    What is purchasing power parity?

    Valuing every country's output at the same prices, so comparisons reflect what incomes actually buy. Without it, poor countries with cheap services look poorer than they are.

    What is the Balassa-Samuelson effect?

    Services that cannot be traded are cheaper in poor countries because wages there are low, which makes the overall price level lower. A haircut in India costs under a dollar.

    What is the rule of 70?

    Doubling time is about 70 divided by the growth rate in percent: about 35 years at 2%, about 10 years at 7%.

    What are the key institutions for growth?

    Property rights, honest government, political stability, a dependable legal system, and competitive, open markets.

    Why is being landlocked called a natural tariff?

    High transport costs act like a tax on every import and export: prices inland rise, trade shrinks and specialization suffers.

    14. Answer templates

    Define GDP

    “GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. Final means we skip intermediate inputs to avoid double counting; produced means resales and transfers do not count.”

    Nominal, real, per person

    “Nominal GDP rises with prices. Real GDP holds prices fixed, so it shows whether we produce more. Dividing by population gives real GDP per capita, the closest single measure of how the average person is doing.”

    Comparing countries

    “Tradable goods cost about the same everywhere because of arbitrage, but services cannot be traded and workers cannot move freely. Low wages make services and the whole price level cheaper in poor countries, so we compare incomes at PPP.”

    Growth rates

    “By the rule of 70, 2% growth doubles income every 35 years and 7% every 10. Small gaps compound: Japan went from half of Argentina's living standard in 1950 to twice it by 2015.”

    Why some countries are rich

    “The immediate reason is productive workers with more physical capital, human capital and technology, organized by entrepreneurs. The deeper reason is incentives created by institutions: property rights, honest government, stability, dependable courts and open markets. North and South Korea show how different institutions turn the same starting point into a miracle and a disaster.”
